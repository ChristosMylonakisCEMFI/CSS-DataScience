\begin{frame}{Prediction as an objective}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item A prediction rule maps observed characteristics into an unobserved outcome.
  \item It is estimated on a sample and judged on observations not used to estimate it.
  \item The criterion is accuracy out of sample, not the value of any coefficient.
  \item A model may describe the data poorly and still predict well.
  \item Bias in the coefficients is acceptable if it lowers prediction error.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

\simfig{0.82\textwidth}{fig_overfit_reg}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Fit in the estimation sample improves monotonically with the degree.
  \item With 20 observations, degree 19 passes through every point exactly.
  \item Error in an independent sample is minimized at a low degree.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

\end{tikzpicture}

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Partition the sample into $k$ subsets of equal size.
  \item Estimate on $k-1$ subsets and evaluate on the one left out.
  \item Repeat so each subset serves once, then average the $k$ estimates.
  \item With a binary outcome, folds preserve the proportion of ones.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Where leakage enters}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Transformations computed on the full sample before it is split.
  \item Regressors dated after the outcome is realized.
  \item Any variable constructed from the outcome, including category means.
  \item A fit far better than expected is more often leakage than a good model.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Obtaining an honest estimate of performance}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Split the sample before computing any transformation.
  \item Estimate standardization and imputation on training observations only.
  \item Use the test sample once, after every choice has been made.
  \item Include a regressor only if it is known before the outcome is realized.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Measuring accuracy: continuous outcomes}
\simfig{0.78\textwidth}{fig_rmse_mae}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Root mean squared error weights large errors more heavily.
  \item Mean absolute error weights every unit of error equally.
  \item A few extreme observations move the first much more than the second.
  \item Fix the measure in advance, to reflect the cost of an error.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Measuring accuracy: binary outcomes}
\simfig{0.76\textwidth}{fig_accuracy_trap}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item When ones are rare, the share of correct classifications is uninformative.
  \item Predicting zero everywhere is 96\% accurate and identifies nothing.
  \item The table of predicted against actual outcomes is the useful summary.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Thresholds, precision, and recall}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Most estimators return a predicted probability, not a classification.
  \item Turning it into a classification requires a threshold.
  \item Precision: the share of predicted ones that are actual ones.
  \item Recall: the share of actual ones that are predicted.
  \item Raising the threshold raises precision and lowers recall.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The choice of regressors}
\simfig{0.78\textwidth}{fig_features}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Both panels report least squares on a single regressor.
  \item The outcome is generated by $y = 2\log x + \varepsilon$.
  \item Replacing $x$ by $\log x$ lowers out-of-sample error by an order of magnitude.
  \item How the regressors are built often matters more than the estimator.
\end{wideitemize}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Constructing regressors in practice}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Indicators for categorical variables.
  \item Interactions and ratios.
  \item Logarithms and powers, for skewness and curvature.
  \item Discretization of continuous variables.
  \item Components of dates.
  \item An explicit indicator for missing values.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Two cautions}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Replacing a category by its mean outcome is a common source of leakage.
  \item It has to be computed separately within each training fold.
  \item Regressors with no predictive content raise the variance of the rule.
  \item Adding them is not free, even when their coefficients are near zero.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{A menu of estimators}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Least squares: transparent, and often competitive with good regressors.
  \item Ridge and LASSO: a penalty on the coefficients; LASSO sets some to zero.
  \item Regression tree: a recursive partition; a single tree is unstable.
  \item Random forest: averages trees on resampled data; much lower variance.
  \item Boosting: small trees in sequence, each fitted to the residuals.
  \item Each has a counterpart when the outcome is binary.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Penalized regression}
\simfig{0.73\textwidth}{fig_lasso_path}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item 70 observations, 40 regressors, 3 with nonzero coefficients.
  \item The penalty shrinks coefficients and sets irrelevant ones to zero.
  \item Test error is minimized at a strictly positive penalty.
  \item The penalty is chosen by cross-validation, not by the fit.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Trees and random forests}
\simfig{0.74\textwidth}{fig_tree_rf}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item A single tree's boundary depends heavily on the particular sample.
  \item A forest averages many trees, each estimated on resampled data.
  \item Flexibility is retained while the variance is substantially reduced.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Boosting}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Trees are added in sequence rather than averaged in parallel.
  \item Each is fitted to the residuals of the current model.
  \item Each enters with a small weight, so progress is gradual.
  \item It is typically the most accurate of the estimators considered here.
  \item It has more parameters, and overfits if they are chosen carelessly.
  \item A random forest needs fewer choices and is a good starting point.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Comparing estimators}
\simfig{0.78\textwidth}{fig_compare}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item The same regressors and the same folds for every estimator.
  \item The flexible estimators win here: the data have curvature and interaction.
  \item On data close to linear the ordering is frequently reversed.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Summary}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item Predictive accuracy concerns observations not used in estimation.
  \item Fit in the estimation sample does not establish it.
  \item The relevant trade-off is between bias and variance.
  \item Cross-validation is the standard way of estimating that accuracy.
  \item Fix the measure of accuracy in advance, by the cost of an error.
  \item The construction of regressors often matters more than the estimator.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The decomposition of expected prediction error}
For a new observation at $x_0$, with $y = f(x_0) + \varepsilon$ and $\hat f$ estimated on a
random sample,
\[
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[(y - \hat f(x_0))^2\right]
  = \big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\big)^2
  + \operatorname{Var}\!\big(\hat f(x_0)\big)
  + \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon).
\]
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item The expectation is taken over samples, not over observations.
  \item The first two terms can be traded against one another.
  \item The third cannot be reduced by any estimator.
  \item Penalization and averaging lower the second at some cost in the first.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Boosting: successive fits}
\simfig{0.80\textwidth}{fig_boosting}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item A single tree of depth two is a coarse step function.
  \item Successive trees fitted to the residuals accumulate into an accurate fit.
  \item Continued far enough, the procedure begins to fit the noise.
  \item The number of trees is itself chosen by cross-validation.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Further measures for binary outcomes}
\begin{wideitemize}
  \item When ones are rare, the precision--recall curve is more informative.
  \item ROC can look favourable when few of the predicted ones are correct.
  \item The $F_1$ measure combines precision and recall in one number.
  \item Log-loss penalizes confident predictions that turn out to be wrong.
  \item Calibration: of those given probability $0.7$, about 70\% should be ones.
\end{wideitemize}
\end{frame}
